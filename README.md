## Introduction

[Ergodic literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_literature) is Espen J. Aarseth's term, from _Cybertext_ (1997), for texts in which "nontrivial effort is required to allow the reader to traverse the text." Existing resources mostly cover digital work. This list focuses on print and describes each work by its reading mechanics: footnote mazes, unbound pages, die-cut paper, alternate reading orders, and so on.

Each work appears once below, under its dominant mechanic, and links to its page on the website with full metadata.

### Footnote maze

The reading path splits across notes, appendices, and apparatus.

- [Ada, or Ardor](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/ada-or-ardor/) - Vladimir Nabokov, 1969. Ends with notes by an anagrammatic editor, Vivian Darkbloom; the reader moves between the text and notes to catch the novel’s multilingual puns and references.
- [Codex Unfertho](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/codex-unfertho/) - Kienn Nguyen, 2026. Two vanished scholars leave a labyrinth of notes, footnotes, ciphers, and redactions; the reader deciphers them and may cut, tear, or fold the pages. Also: physical intervention, typographic.
- [House of Leaves](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/house-of-leaves/) - Mark Z. Danielewski, 2000. The reader follows nested footnotes, appendices, and an index across several narrators, and has to turn the book, read mirrored text, and track passages that thin to a few words per page. Also: typographic.
- [Infinite Jest](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/infinite-jest/) - David Foster Wallace, 1996. Hundreds of endnotes, some with their own notes, carry essential plot; readers famously keep two bookmarks and flip back and forth.
- [Lolita](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/lolita/) - Vladimir Nabokov, 1955. Framed as a confession with a fictional editor’s foreword; read in order, but the narrator’s anagrams, allusions, and planted clues reward rereading.
- [Pale Fire](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/pale-fire/) - Vladimir Nabokov, 1962. A 999-line poem arrives wrapped in a foreword, line-by-line commentary, and index by an unreliable editor; the story emerges as the reader chases cross-references between poem, notes, and index.
- [The Quincunx](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-quincunx/) - Charles Palliser, 1989. A Victorian inheritance mystery built on a five-part pattern, with genealogical tables the reader consults to untangle who is related to whom. Read in order.
- [The Selected Works of T.S. Spivet](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-selected-works-of-ts-spivet/) - Reif Larsen, 2009. Maps, diagrams, and side notes fill the margins, linked to the main text by drawn lines; the reader keeps leaving the story to follow them. Also: typographic.
- [Viljevo (August After Midnight)](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/viljevo/) - Luka Bekavac, 2013. Three parts: a transcript of a damaged tape, records of otherworldly radio transmissions, and a mock scholarly article with footnotes; the reader assembles one story across them. Also: typographic.

### Multiple paths

Author-defined alternate orders or reading routes.

- [Crossings](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/crossings/) - Alex Landragin, 2020. Three stories can be read straight through, or in the alternate chapter order given at the front, which tells a different story.
- [Hopscotch (Rayuela)](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/hopscotch/) - Julio Cortázar, 1963. A table of instructions offers two books: read chapters 1–56 in order, or start at chapter 73 and hop through all 155 chapters following the number printed at the end of each.
- [If on a winter's night a traveler](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/if-on-a-winters-night-a-traveler/) - Italo Calvino, 1979. Second-person chapters about “you, the Reader” alternate with the openings of ten different novels, each broken off; the reader assembles the frame story across the interruptions.
- [Landscape Painted with Tea](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/landscape-painted-with-tea/) - Milorad Pavić, 1988. Subtitled a novel for crossword lovers: chapters are labeled Across and Down, and the reader can read straight through or follow the crossword’s directions.
- [Milo and the Magical Stones](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/milo-and-the-magical-stones/) - Marcus Pfister, 1997. Halfway through, the pages split horizontally into two stacks, one with a happy ending and one with a sad ending, and the reader chooses which to follow. Also: physical intervention.
- [Only Revolutions](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/only-revolutions/) - Mark Z. Danielewski, 2006. Two narrators’ accounts start from opposite covers, printed upside down relative to each other; the reader flips the book back and forth, usually eight pages at a time, alongside marginal chronology columns. Also: typographic.
- [Oryx and Crake](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/oryx-and-crake/) - Margaret Atwood, 2003. Alternates between a post-apocalyptic present and the past that led to it. Read in order; no mechanic in this list’s vocabulary really fits.

### Reader-assembled

Loose-leaf, boxed, shuffleable, or unbound.

- [Building Stories](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/building-stories/) - Chris Ware, 2012. A box of fourteen printed pieces (books, pamphlets, broadsheets, a fold-out board) with no prescribed order; the reader chooses the sequence.
- [Cain's Jawbone](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/cains-jawbone/) - E. Powys Mathers, 1934. One hundred pages printed deliberately out of order; the reader must put them back in sequence and name six murderers and their victims.
- [Composition No. 1](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/composition-no-1/) - Marc Saporta, 1962. About 150 unbound, unnumbered pages come in a box; the reader shuffles them and reads in whatever order results.
- [Float](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/float/) - Anne Carson, 2016. Twenty-two chapbooks loose in a clear case, with no prescribed order; the reader decides what to read and when.
- [The Researcher's First Murder](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-researchers-first-murder/) - John Finnemore, 2024. A new puzzle in the style of Cain’s Jawbone: the pages are out of order, and the reader must reorder them to solve the case.
- [The Unfortunates](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-unfortunates/) - B. S. Johnson, 1969. Twenty-seven separately bound sections come in a box; the first and last are marked, and the reader reads the rest in any order.

### Permutational

Combinatorial text, such as cut strips or generated combinations.

- [Cent mille milliards de poèmes](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/cent-mille-milliards-de-poemes/) - Raymond Queneau, 1961. Ten sonnets are printed with each line on a separate cut strip; the reader turns strips independently to compose any of 10^14 possible sonnets. Also: physical intervention.
- [Subcutanean](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/subcutanean/) - Aaron A. Reed, 2020. Generated so that every printed copy is different; each reader gets their own version of the story.
- [Tristano](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/tristano/) - Nanni Balestrini, 1966. Paragraphs were shuffled by computer so that every printed copy has a different arrangement; each reader holds a unique version.

### Lexicon

Dictionary or encyclopedia structure, read in any order.

- [Dictionary of the Khazars](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/dictionary-of-the-khazars/) - Milorad Pavić, 1984. Three cross-referenced encyclopedias (Christian, Islamic, and Jewish) tell overlapping versions of one event; the reader picks any entry and follows symbols between books. Published in “male” and “female” editions that differ by one paragraph. Also: multiple paths.
- [Unlanguage](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/unlanguage/) - Michael Cisco, 2018. Written as a course in a strange language, with lessons and exercises; the story of a suicide survivor emerges as the reader works through them.

### Typographic

Layout, rotation, color, or spacing is part of the traversal.

- [Betrayals](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/betrayals/) - Charles Palliser, 1994. Linked pieces in different forms (an obituary, a review, letters, a crossword); the reader assembles a hidden conspiracy from connections between them.
- [Chasing Homer](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/chasing-homer/) - László Krasznahorkai, 2019. Each chapter pairs with a painting and a recorded drum track meant to be heard while reading, so the pace of reading is set by music outside the book.
- [Dhalgren](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/dhalgren/) - Samuel R. Delany, 1975. Ends mid-sentence with words that complete the first line, making the novel a loop; the final chapter splits into parallel columns of a notebook the reader must interleave.
- [Double or Nothing](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/double-or-nothing/) - Raymond Federman, 1971. Every page is typographically arranged, with words in columns, shapes, and patterns; the reader has to work out the reading path on each page.
- [Drift](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/drift/) - Caroline Bergvall, 2014. A sequence based on the Old English poem The Seafarer, where spelling, letterforms, and spacing break down and drift across the page.
- [Ella Minnow Pea](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/ella-minnow-pea/) - Mark Dunn, 2001. An epistolary novel in which letters of the alphabet are banned one by one and vanish from the text, so the prose grows harder to read as the story goes on. Read in order.
- [Extremely Loud & Incredibly Close](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/extremely-loud-and-incredibly-close/) - Jonathan Safran Foer, 2005. Photographs, blank pages, red-circled corrections, and overprinted pages interrupt the text, ending with a flip-book the reader runs backward through.
- [Follow This Thread](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/follow-this-thread/) - Henry Eliot, 2018. A history of mazes laid out as one: a thread runs across every page, and the reader follows it through puzzles and branching paths.
- [Here](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/here/) - Richard McGuire, 2014. Every spread shows the same corner of one room, with inset panels from different years layered over it; the reader moves across centuries within a single page.
- [Horrorstör](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/horrorstor/) - Grady Hendrix, 2014. Designed as a flat-pack furniture store catalog, with product pages that grow more sinister chapter by chapter.
- [Illuminae](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/illuminae/) - Amie Kaufman & Jay Kristoff, 2015. Told entirely through a dossier of hacked files (chat logs, emails, schematics, AI transcripts), with text set in spirals and shapes; the reader reconstructs events across formats.
- [Nibiru](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/nibiru/) - Karl Tenbro, 2019. A hallucinatory noir with reversed sentences, shifting case, children’s drawings, and reproduced paintings set into the text.
- [Night Film](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/night-film/) - Marisha Pessl, 2013. A thriller interleaved with mocked-up web pages, police reports, and photographs; a companion app once unlocked extra material from images in the book. Read in order.
- [Parabola](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/parabola/) - Lily Hoang, 2008. Moves along the curve of a parabola, mixing stories with mathematical formulas and photographs that the reader must read as part of the narrative.
- [Scorch Atlas](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/scorch-atlas/) - Blake Butler, 2009. Stories of strange apocalypses in a book printed to look water-stained and decaying, with pages of grids, lists, and blacked-out text.
- [The Raw Shark Texts](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-raw-shark-texts/) - Steven Hall, 2007. A conceptual shark made of words swims through the text; the reader watches it form out of typography and flips a long sequence of pages to see it move toward them.
- [The Scrapbook of Frankie Pratt](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-scrapbook-of-frankie-pratt/) - Caroline Preston, 2011. A novel told entirely as a 1920s scrapbook of vintage ephemera with typewritten captions; the reader pieces the story together from the collage.
- [The Supernatural Enhancements](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-supernatural-enhancements/) - Edgar Cantero, 2014. Told through diaries, letters, transcripts, and diagrams, with a cipher puzzle the reader can try to solve alongside the characters. Read in order.
- [The Tunnel](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-tunnel/) - William H. Gass, 1995. A historian’s digressive confession, broken up by cartoons, shaped text, and shifting typefaces that the reader has to parse as part of the story.
- [The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-wind-up-bird-chronicle/) - Haruki Murakami, 1994. A novel that folds in letters, war stories, and computer chat transcripts. Read in order; no mechanic in this list’s vocabulary really fits.
- [theMystery.doc](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/themystery-doc/) - Matthew McIntosh, 2017. More than 1,600 pages of fragments, transcripts, photographs, blank pages, and screenshots; the reader assembles a story from a document that keeps changing form.
- [Unflattening](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/unflattening/) - Nick Sousanis, 2015. A doctoral dissertation drawn as comics, where panel layout carries the argument. Read in order.
- [Voices: The Final Hours of Joan of Arc](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/voices-the-final-hours-of-joan-of-arc/) - David Elliott, 2019. Joan of Arc’s story told by people and objects around her in medieval verse forms, with some poems shaped like their speakers on the page.
- [We Used to Live Here](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/we-used-to-live-here/) - Marcus Kliewer, 2024. A horror novel interleaved with found documents; Morse code at the end of each interlude and stray capital letters spell hidden messages for the reader to decode. Otherwise read in order.
- [XX](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/xx/) - Rian Hughes, 2020. A novel about a signal from deep space, built from documents, transcripts, and pages where typeface and layout carry the story.
- [Z213: Exit](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/z213-exit/) - Dimitris Lyacos, 2009. A fugitive’s diary in broken fragments, with gaps and bracketed omissions; the reader reconstructs the journey from what remains.

### Physical intervention

Die-cut pages, inserts, objects, or ephemera.

- [Bats of the Republic](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/bats-of-the-republic/) - Zachary Thomas Dodson, 2015. Two stories set two centuries apart, told through letters, maps, and specimen drawings, ending with a sealed envelope bound into the book that the story warns you not to open. Also: typographic.
- [Nox](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/nox/) - Anne Carson, 2010. A single accordion-folded sheet in a box, reproducing an elegy scrapbook; dictionary entries for each word of a Latin poem run alongside photographs and fragments. Also: lexicon.
- [The Strange Library](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-strange-library/) - Haruki Murakami, 2005. The illustrated English edition, designed by Chip Kidd, opens with a folding flap like a library pamphlet and mixes found images into the story, so handling the object is part of reading it.
- [Tree of Codes](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/tree-of-codes/) - Jonathan Safran Foer, 2010. Die-cut from an English translation of Bruno Schulz’s The Street of Crocodiles; holes in each page expose words on the pages beneath, so the reader reads through layers of paper.

### Gamebook

Branching choices with explicit instructions to the reader.

- [If](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/if-nicholas-bourbaki/) - Nicholas Bourbaki, 2014. The reader is the second-person protagonist and makes a decision at the end of each chapter, steering toward one of twenty-two endings.
- [Meanwhile](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/meanwhile/) - Jason Shiga, 2010. A branching comic: the reader follows tubes from panel to panel and jumps between pages using printed tabs, through thousands of possible stories. Also: physical intervention.
- [Multiple Choice](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/multiple-choice/) - Alejandro Zambra, 2014. Modeled on Chile’s university entrance exam; the reader answers multiple-choice, sentence-ordering, and reading-comprehension questions, and the answers shape what the stories mean.
- [The Paradox Paradox](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/the-paradox-paradox/) - Daniel Hardcastle, 2025. A choose-your-path adventure for adults: numbered sections and choices, with time paradoxes that loop the reader back on themselves.
- [You Are Reading These Words](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/you-are-reading-these-words/) - David de Léon, 2026. The book addresses the reader directly and sets fourteen exercises to perform, some in a chair and some out in the world; the reading happens in the doing.

### Hypertext

Linked nodes, digital or simulated in print.

- [253](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/253/) - Geoff Ryman, 1996. Each of 253 passengers on a London Underground train gets exactly 253 words; the reader moves between passengers through links (on the web) or cross-references (in the 1998 print remix).
- [afternoon, a story](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/afternoon-a-story/) - Michael Joyce, 1987. A Storyspace hypertext in which the reader chooses words that “yield” to other passages; conditional links change what appears based on the path already taken.
- [Pry](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/pry/) - Samantha Gorman & Danny Cannizzaro, 2014. A touchscreen novella: the reader pinches a character’s eyes open to see the world, or pries apart lines of text to reach his buried thoughts.
- [Twelve Blue](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/twelve-blue/) - Michael Joyce, 1996. A web hypertext of twelve story threads; links in the text disappear once followed, and the reader weaves between threads shown as colored lines.

### Annotated dialogue

Multiple readers' annotations form a parallel narrative.

- [S.](https://ergodic.rrbaker.com/works/s/) - J. J. Abrams & Doug Dorst, 2013. A fictional library novel carries two readers’ handwritten marginal conversation, layered by ink color across time, plus loose inserts; the reader decides how to interleave text, margins, and ephemera. Also: physical intervention.
